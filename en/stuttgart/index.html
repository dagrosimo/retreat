Joseph Süß Oppenheimer: Faith vs. Absolutism

Offered survival if he accepted baptism, Duke Karl Alexander’s former finance director refused to betray his ancestors’ faith and died in an iron cage on Stuttgart’s Galgenberg—exposing the hidden antisemitism beneath 18th-century courtly splendor.

Stuttgart’s 18th century opens with two stark tests of conscience. After Duke Karl Alexander’s death in 1737, treasury reformer Joseph Süß Oppenheimer was targeted by entrenched antisemitism; refusing baptism as the price of survival, he kept his faith and was executed in 1738.

Forty years later, journalist C. F. D. Schubart exposed Württemberg’s mercenary-soldier trade in his “Deutsche Chronik” and spent ten years imprisoned without trial in Hohenasperg fortress—where a young, awestruck Friedrich Schiller visited him in secret.

StadtPalais Stuttgart · Galgenberg memorial · Festung Hohenasperg · Hospitalkirche

Inspired by Schubart while trapped inside the rigid Karlsschule military academy, Schiller published “The Robbers” (1781). Placed under arrest and barred from writing anything but medicine, he fled Stuttgart under cover of a court festival in September 1782.

Decades later, Romantic prodigy Wilhelm Hauff (1802–1827) packed into just 24 years his ethical fairy tales, the Swabian novel “Lichtenstein,” and the novella “Jud Süß”—returning compassionately to Oppenheimer’s tragedy.

Altes Schloss · Karlsschule · Schiller-Nationalmuseum Marbach · Hoppenlau-Friedhof · Schloss Lichtenstein

In the 1830s, Stuttgart split over Germany’s first monument to Schiller: boyhood friend Johann Heinrich von Dannecker sculpted a fiery rebel, yet the committee chose Danish classicist Bertel Thorvaldsen’s serene draped sage (1839).

Soon Romantic longing erupted into open revolt: poet Georg Herwegh electrified youth with “Poems of a Living Man” (1841) and led the German Democratic Legion across the Rhine into the 1848 uprising.

Schillerplatz · Staatsgalerie (Dannecker’s bust) · Haus der Geschichte · Calwer Straße

Stuttgart’s greatest paradox is its native son G. W. F. Hegel (1770–1831). Though seen in Berlin as an official conservative, his “Phenomenology of Spirit” hid a dialectical time-bomb that Young Hegelians turned into the intellectual weapon of 1848.

Poet Eduard Mörike closes the circle: after his tragic “Painter Nolten,” his 1855 novella “Mozart on the Way to Prague” carries us back to autumn 1787—the very 18th-century world of Oppenheimer, Schubart, and young Schiller—turning raw history into luminous reflection.
