The Granite Bowl in the Lustgarten

Lustgarten · Am Alten Museum, 10178 Berlin
Architecture & Site History

Installed before the Altes Museum in 1831, the 75-ton granite basin anchored Hummel’s triptych and Berlin’s earliest daguerreotypes.

On-Site Philosophical Practice

Day 2 live observational practice: matching real reflections across the granite rim with Hummel’s canvas.

Key Site Artifacts:

Before encountering the monolith in the Lustgarten, we examine Schinkel’s early architectural paintings—strict classical proportions casting Berlin as an ideal ‘new Acropolis’.

Next, Hummel’s mirror geometry (‘The Chess Players’) culminates in his 1831 Granite Bowl triptych: quarrying, polishing, and installing the stone as a visionary mirror of the cosmos.

Schinkel architects Berlin’s ideal center; Hummel turns the polished granite monolith into a metaphysical mirror.

Standing before the originals inside Alte Nationalgalerie reveals the core thesis: 19th-century sacred Berlin existed only inside the optical discipline of four masters—Schinkel, Hummel, Krüger, and Gaertner.

Krüger binds the monolith to civic ritual on Opernplatz, while Gaertner’s rooftop panorama gathers cathedral spires, the Spree, and streets into one sunlit whole.

Krüger anchors the sacred stone in civic ceremony on Opernplatz; Gaertner unifies Berlin from the church rooftop.

First, 1840s daguerreotypists Hermann Biow and Carl Gustav Oehme reduce Hummel’s ‘cosmic mirror’ to weathered granite dust. In the 1850s, Leopold Ahrendts catalogs Schinkel’s buildings as bare structural inventory.

By the 1870s Friedrich Albert Schwartz submerges the square in carriages and billboards, and by the 1880s Otto Braun completes the clinical archival survey—ending Painting’s monopoly.

Forty-eight years of camera conquest: Biow & Oehme (1840s) → Ahrendts (1850s) → Schwartz (1870s) → Otto Braun (1880s).

Operating on ground cleared by the camera, Adolph Menzel breaks with earlier magic realism—shifting from the Berlin-Potsdam Railway (1847) to the molten industrial weight of the Iron Rolling Mill (1872–1875).

On the quiet morning of Day 5 behind closed doors, the group synthesizes the four-day optical journey—each guest departing with a small physical keepsake of the Granite Bowl.

Where camera inventory dismantled Romantic enchantment, Menzel answers with heat and weight (‘Iron Rolling Mill’)—culminating in our silent Day 5 Closed Room gathering.
